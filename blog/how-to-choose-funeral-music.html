Guide — Funeral Music

How to Choose the Music
for a Funeral

What Entrance Music, Reflection Music and Exit Music really mean — and how to choose songs that sound like the person they’re for.

My Life in 3 Songs  ·  April 2025  ·  9 min read

Music at a funeral does something words cannot. It arrives before the grief has been spoken aloud, fills silences that would otherwise feel unbearable, and stays with people long after the day itself. Ask someone about a funeral they attended years ago and they are far more likely to remember the music than the eulogy.

Yet when a family sits down to choose songs — often in the days immediately after a death, under impossible pressure — the question of where to even begin can feel overwhelming. What kind of music is appropriate? How many songs do you need? What is entrance music, exactly, and how is it different from reflection music or exit music?

This guide answers all of those questions practically and honestly. It is written both for people planning a funeral for someone else, and for people who want to record their own wishes in advance — which, as we will discuss, is one of the most considered gifts you can give to the people you love.

♩

Why music matters so much at a funeral

Music is not decoration at a funeral. It is structure. It tells people when to settle, when to grieve, when to breathe, and when it is time to leave. A well-chosen piece of music can communicate the entire character of a person in three minutes — their humour, their tenderness, the era they belonged to, the things they loved.

Research into the psychology of grief consistently finds that music helps people access emotions they might otherwise suppress. A song triggers the limbic system — the part of the brain that handles memory and feeling — in a way that spoken language simply does not. This is why a song can make a room full of strangers cry simultaneously, and why the right piece of music at a funeral can feel like the most honest thing said all day.

“Music provides emotional punctuation. It lets people cry when they need to, smile when they can, and connect when words fall short.”

The structure of a funeral service — whether religious or secular, traditional or a celebration of life — typically creates three distinct musical moments. Understanding what each moment is for makes the process of choosing songs considerably easier.

01

Entrance Music

As you arrive — the first impression

Entrance Music is played as the coffin is carried into the venue and as guests take their seats. It is the first thing people hear, and it sets the emotional tone for everything that follows. This is arguably the most important musical choice of the three.

The entrance is a moment of arrival and acknowledgement — the room recognising that this person was here, and now is not. The music needs to carry that weight while also giving people something to hold onto. It should feel like the person it is for.

There is no rule that says entrance music must be slow or solemn. A person who spent their life being joyful deserves entrance music that reflects that. What matters is authenticity — that the people in the room hear the first notes and think, yes, that sounds like them.

Commonly chosen entrance songs
Time to Say Goodbye — Andrea Bocelli Ave Maria — Schubert Abide With Me — Traditional My Way — Frank Sinatra Gabriel’s Oboe — Ennio Morricone Somewhere Over the Rainbow — Israel Kamakawiwo‘ole
Choosing entrance music — things to consider
  • What was their all-time favourite song? If it works in this context, it often belongs here.
  • What genre or era did they love? The sound itself — even before the lyrics — should feel like them.
  • How long does the entrance take? A short processional needs a shorter piece, or a track that can fade gracefully.
  • If the service is religious, check with the venue or officiant that the chosen track is permitted.
♩
02

Reflection Music

During the service — the heart of the ceremony

Reflection Music is played during the service itself — typically accompanying a photo tribute, a slideshow, a moment of contemplation, or the committal (when the coffin is taken away). It is the most intimate of the three musical moments, and the one that tends to stay with people longest.

Where entrance music announces, reflection music remembers. It gives the room permission to feel whatever they are feeling. It does not need to be slow, but it does need to be honest. The best reflection music feels as though it was written specifically for the person being remembered — even when it was not.

Not every service includes a photo tribute or slideshow, and that is perfectly fine. If the service is more traditional, reflection music simply plays during a quiet moment of collective remembrance. The effect is the same: it creates a space where grief does not have to be explained or performed.

Commonly chosen reflection songs
Supermarket Flowers — Ed Sheeran Candle in the Wind — Elton John Angels — Robbie Williams Hallelujah — Jeff Buckley The Living Years — Mike + The Mechanics Wind Beneath My Wings — Bette Midler Nimrod — Elgar

“A reflection track doesn’t have to be slow, and a farewell song doesn’t have to be sombre. The most moving ceremonies are those that sound like the person they’re for.”

Choosing reflection music — things to consider
  • Will there be a photo tribute or slideshow? If so, the track length needs to match the running time.
  • Read the lyrics carefully before committing. A song can sound perfect but contain a line that lands unexpectedly hard.
  • Think about what you want people to feel in this moment — comfort, gratitude, grief, joy? Let that guide the choice.
  • Instrumental versions of well-known songs can work beautifully here if the original lyrics feel too on-the-nose.
♩
03

Exit Music

As you leave — the final word

Exit Music plays as guests leave the venue. It is the last impression — the final note of the day — and it carries more emotional weight than people expect. After an hour of grief and remembrance, the right exit song can lift the room, release it, or send people out with exactly the feeling the person would have wanted to leave them with.

Exit music is often where families feel most free to be personal, even playful. If the person had a favourite song they would have wanted blasting at full volume, this is usually where it belongs. Many families choose something distinctly upbeat for the exit — a statement that this person lived fully, loved music, and would not have wanted their final moment to be purely sorrowful.

Some of the most memorable exit choices are ones that make the congregation smile through tears — a beloved pop song, an unexpected classic, something that makes people turn to each other and say, yes, that is exactly right.

Commonly chosen exit songs
Always Look on the Bright Side of Life — Monty Python What a Wonderful World — Louis Armstrong I Will Always Love You — Whitney Houston Don’t Stop Me Now — Queen Highway to Hell — AC/DC Simply the Best — Tina Turner Dancing Queen — ABBA
Choosing exit music — things to consider
  • What is the last feeling you want people to carry with them? That should guide the tone entirely.
  • Exit music can be more personal and surprising than entrance music — this is where character shows.
  • Upbeat exit songs are increasingly common and entirely appropriate — do not feel constrained by tradition.
  • Consider how the song ends. Guests will still be filing out when it finishes — does it loop gracefully, or need to be faded?

Practical things families often don’t know

Music licensing. Streaming directly from Spotify or YouTube is generally not permitted at funeral venues, as it breaches copyright. Most funeral homes and crematoria use licensed systems that can play virtually any commercial track legally. If you are arranging music yourself, check with the venue about what formats they accept — most now take digital files rather than physical media.

Live music. If a family member or friend plays an instrument or sings, a live performance can be profoundly moving. If you are considering hiring a professional musician, your funeral director will usually have recommendations. Bear in mind that live performances require coordination with the venue and a brief rehearsal beforehand.

Religious services. Some religious traditions have specific rules about what music can be played. Catholic services often include at least three songs, sometimes drawn from a pre-approved list. Jewish funerals traditionally include very little music. If you are unsure what is permitted, speak with the officiant early in the planning process.

How many songs do you need? Most services include three to five pieces of music. Three is the standard — one for each key moment — and is enough for a meaningful, complete service. Additional songs can be added for longer services or where live performance is involved, but three carefully chosen pieces will almost always be sufficient.

The case for choosing your own songs in advance

Everything above is written from the perspective of a family planning a funeral under time pressure, in grief, trying to represent someone they love. It is hard. Even when families know the person well, there is always uncertainty: would they have wanted this? Would they have hated that?

The simplest way to remove that uncertainty is to record your own wishes while you can.

It does not require a solicitor, a formal document, or even a particularly long conversation. It requires three songs and a few minutes. Entrance Music, Reflection Music, Exit Music. The songs that sound like you — chosen by you, when you have the time and clarity to think about it properly, rather than by people you love who are doing their best under impossible circumstances.

David Bowie planned his own farewell with deliberate care. The Queen discussed her service decades in advance. Most people don’t have that kind of notice — but the act of recording your wishes costs nothing except a little thought, and it gives your family something invaluable: certainty.

“The most moving ceremonies are those that sound like the person they’re for. And the only person who truly knows what that sounds like is you.”
