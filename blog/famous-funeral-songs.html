Editorial — Famous Funerals

Songs Played at Famous Funerals

From Elton John’s once-in-a-lifetime performance for Princess Diana to David Bowie’s quiet, private farewell — the music that marked history’s most significant goodbyes.

My Life in 3 Songs  ·  April 2026  ·  8 min read

Music at a funeral does something words cannot. It cuts through grief, bypasses the rational mind, and arrives somewhere deeper — somewhere that remembers. The songs chosen for history’s most famous farewells tell us something profound not just about the person who died, but about the people who loved them and the moment in time they shared.

Some of these choices were made by the person themselves, long in advance. Others were selected under impossible time pressure, by grieving families trying to distil an entire life into three pieces of music. All of them became part of the story.

♩
01

Princess Diana

Westminster Abbey — 6th September 1997

Two billion people watched. In the days after Diana’s death in Paris, her family and the palace had to plan a funeral in under a week — one that somehow honoured both the formal weight of the monarchy and the woman who had broken almost every royal convention. Music was central to navigating that impossible tension.

Entrance
I Vow to Thee, My Country
Traditional hymn — Diana’s own favourite, also sung at her wedding
During service
Candle in the Wind 1997 — “Goodbye England’s Rose”
Elton John, performed live — the only public performance he ever gave of this version
Exit
Bach: Prelude in C minor, BWV 546
Organ voluntary as the coffin left Westminster Abbey

The defining moment was Elton John’s performance. Originally written as a tribute to Marilyn Monroe in 1973, the song was rewritten in just hours by lyricist Bernie Taupin after Richard Branson reportedly passed on a request from the Spencer family. John has never performed it again publicly, calling it a song for that single occasion.

“At the time, it brought together mourners all over the UK. It still has the power to connect people in times of grief.”

In the numbers

“Candle in the Wind 1997” sold 658,000 copies on its first day of release — the fastest-selling single in UK chart history at the time. It has sold over 33 million copies worldwide, with all royalties donated to Diana’s charities.

The rest of the service balanced the traditional with the personal: four hymns Diana herself had loved, John Tavener’s ethereal “Song for Athene” sung as the coffin was carried out, and a congregation of 2,000 that included heads of state from around the world, united in silence.

02

Queen Elizabeth II

Westminster Abbey — 19th September 2022

The first state funeral in Britain since Winston Churchill in 1965, the Queen’s service was the result of decades of careful planning — much of it by the Queen herself, who was known to have strong and personal musical tastes that extended well beyond the formal Anglican tradition.

Opening hymn
The Day Thou Gavest, Lord, Is Ended
Traditional — a favourite of the Queen, associated with the Commonwealth
Personal choice
The Lord’s My Shepherd
Also sung at her wedding to Prince Philip in 1947 and at her father’s funeral
Closing
Sleep, Dearie, Sleep
Traditional Scottish lament, played on bagpipes by the Sovereign’s Piper as the coffin left

Two new pieces were specially commissioned for the service: a setting of Psalm 42 by Judith Weir, the first female Master of the King’s Music, and a choral anthem by Sir James MacMillan. Both were written in the weeks following her death. This combination of centuries-old tradition and brand new music, written expressly for a single occasion, is uniquely royal — and uniquely moving.

The Queen’s actual favourite songs — revealed in a 2016 radio documentary — included Rogers and Hammerstein’s “Oklahoma!” and various show tunes. These were, perhaps wisely, not included in the state service, though they tell a warmer story about the woman behind the crown.

A detail worth knowing

The “Funeral Sentences” — lines of scripture set to music by William Croft in the 1700s, sung as the coffin enters — have been performed at every British state funeral since the early 18th century. The same words, the same music, for over 300 years.

03

David Bowie

New York, January 2016 — private cremation

David Bowie died on 10 January 2016, two days after the release of his final album Blackstar — an album now widely understood as a deliberate farewell, filled with imagery of death and transformation. He had kept his cancer diagnosis private for 18 months.

His final wishes were as unconventional as his life: no public funeral, no grand ceremony. He was cremated privately in New York, without a traditional service. His death was, as his producer Tony Visconti put it, “a work of art” — planned, deliberate, on his own terms.

“He always did what he wanted to do. And he wanted to do it his way. His death was no different from his life — a work of art.”

— Tony Visconti, producer

Instead of a funeral, Bowie’s farewell happened spontaneously on the streets of Brixton, the south London neighbourhood where he was born. Thousands gathered at the Aladdin Sane mural, singing his songs through the night — “Space Oddity”, “Heroes”, “Starman”. Brixton became, for one evening, a Bowie festival.

His farewell
Lazarus (from Blackstar)
Released two days before his death — “Look up here, I’m in heaven”

Bowie’s story is a powerful reminder of why recording your wishes matters. His family knew what he wanted because he had told them. The private, undramatic farewell he chose was entirely intentional — and only possible because he had planned it. Most people don’t get 18 months’ notice.

04

Whitney Houston

New Hope Baptist Church, Newark — 18th February 2012

Whitney Houston grew up singing in the New Hope Baptist Church, Newark, where her mother Cissy was the choir director. It was where she found her voice. It was, fittingly, where her family chose to say goodbye.

Opening
A Change Is Gonna Come (reworked)
Kim Burrell, with lyrics rewritten to reflect Houston’s life
Tribute
Ribbon in the Sky (rewritten for Whitney)
Stevie Wonder, who adapted the lyrics: “An angel from God’s choir of love”
As guests left
I Will Always Love You
Whitney Houston’s own recording — played as her casket was removed from the church

The nearly four-hour service included performances by Alicia Keys, R. Kelly, and the Winans family. Kevin Costner gave a eulogy that stopped the room. And then, as the casket was carried out, her voice filled the church one last time — that unmistakeable recording of “I Will Always Love You.” There was no performance that could have matched it.

♩

What these choices tell us

Every funeral in this piece is different in scale, culture, and circumstance. But they share something: the music was deliberate. Someone, at some point, had thought about what mattered. Elton John was asked directly by the Spencer family. The Queen had discussed her service decades in advance. David Bowie planned his own absence. Whitney’s family chose the most obvious, most perfect song imaginable — and played her own voice, one last time.

The music you choose for your own funeral tells a story. It tells people who you were, what you loved, what you believed, and how you want to be remembered. It’s one of the most personal decisions you can make — and one of the most generous gifts you can give to the people you leave behind.

They won’t have to guess.
